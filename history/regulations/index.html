Formula 1 history

History of F1 regulations

Formula 1 is a 'formula': a set of rules every car must follow. The word comes from those rules. The FIA, the governing body, writes them in three books.

The technical regulations say how a car may be built: engine size, weight, wing sizes, safety structures. The sporting regulations say how a weekend runs: qualifying, points, penalties, pit stops. Since 2021 the financial regulations also limit how much a team may spend.

Every few years the rules change a lot, usually for one of four reasons: cars had become too fast or dangerous, races had become dull, costs had run away, or car makers wanted engines closer to road cars. Each big change resets the order, and the team that reads the new rules best often dominates for years. This page walks through every era from 1946 to the plans beyond 2026.

1946–1949: Before the championship

After the Second World War, Grand Prix racing restarted with a new set of rules called Formula A, which soon became known as Formula One. There was no championship yet: each race stood on its own, and the most important ones were called Grandes Épreuves.

  • Engine: Engines could be 1.5 litres with a supercharger, or 4.5 litres naturally aspirated (no supercharger). This let pre-war cars race alongside new ones.
  • Sporting rules: No points, no champion. Races could also run to other rules.

Supercharged pre-war Alfa Romeos were the class of the field. The formula gave F1 its name and the engine rules for the first World Championship.

Who got it right: Alfa Romeo, with the pre-war 158 'Alfetta', won almost everything it entered.

1950–1953: The first World Championship

In 1950 the FIA created the World Drivers' Championship, with points for the top five in each Grand Prix and one point for the fastest lap. The first round was the British Grand Prix at Silverstone on 13 May 1950. There was no championship for teams yet.

  • Engine: Same engine formula as before: 1.5-litre supercharged or 4.5-litre naturally aspirated.
  • Sporting rules: Points: 8, 6, 4, 3, 2 for the top five, plus 1 for fastest lap. Only a driver's best four results counted (dropped scores).
  • Safety: Crash helmets became compulsory in 1952.
  • Engine: In 1952 and 1953 the championship was run to Formula Two rules (2-litre engines), because too few F1 cars were left once Alfa Romeo withdrew.

Alfa Romeo won both of the first two titles, with Nino Farina and Juan Manuel Fangio. When the championship switched to Formula Two rules, Ferrari was ready and Alberto Ascari won nine races in a row.

Who got it right: Alfa Romeo in 1950–51, then Ferrari and Ascari in 1952–53.

1954–1960: 2.5 litres and the rear-engine revolution

New engine rules arrived: 2.5 litres without a supercharger (or a tiny 750 cc with one, which nobody used). Mercedes came in with a beautifully engineered car, then left after a terrible accident at Le Mans in 1955. Later in the decade small British teams changed the shape of racing cars by putting the engine behind the driver.

  • Engine: 2.5 litres naturally aspirated, or 750 cc with a supercharger.
  • Engine: From 1958 cars had to use commercial petrol instead of alcohol fuels.
  • Sporting rules: Races were shortened to about 300 km or two hours from 1958. The Constructors' Championship began in 1958.
  • Sporting rules: Shared drives (two drivers splitting one car's points) were banned from 1958.

Fangio won four titles in this period with Mercedes, Ferrari and Maserati. Then Cooper's small rear-engined cars showed that a light, nimble car beat a powerful front-engined one: Jack Brabham won the 1959 and 1960 titles, and within two years every team copied the layout.

Who got it right: Mercedes (1954–55), then Cooper with the rear-engined layout (1959–60).

1961–1965: The 1.5-litre formula

To slow the cars down, engines were cut to 1.5 litres and a minimum weight of 450 kg was set. British teams protested and expected the rule to be dropped, so they were not ready. Ferrari was.

  • Engine: 1.3 to 1.5 litres, no supercharger.
  • Safety: Minimum weight 450 kg, roll bars, double brake circuits, seat-belt mounts and fire protection for fuel tanks became compulsory.
  • Sporting rules: Points became 9, 6, 4, 3, 2, 1 for the top six, and the fastest-lap point had already gone in 1960.

Ferrari's 'sharknose' 156 won the 1961 title for Phil Hill. Then the British fought back with clever chassis: Lotus's 25 of 1962 had a monocoque, a single stiff tub instead of a frame of tubes, and Jim Clark won two titles. The idea is the basis of every F1 car since.

Who got it right: Ferrari in 1961; Lotus and Jim Clark in 1963 and 1965, BRM in 1962.

1966–1976: Return to power

Engines doubled to 3 litres. In 1967 Ford paid Cosworth to build the DFV, a 3-litre V8 that any team could buy, which won races for the next fifteen years. Cars grew wings, teams started painting their cars in sponsors' colours, and slick tyres arrived.

  • Engine: 3 litres naturally aspirated, or 1.5 litres with a supercharger.
  • Aerodynamics: Wings appeared in 1968. Tall wings on stilts kept breaking, so they were banned in 1969 and wing height and width limited.
  • Tyres: Slick tyres (no tread) arrived in 1971 for dry races.
  • Safety: Fuel bladders, fire extinguishers, six-point belts, cockpits a driver could leave in five seconds, guard rails and marshal posts became compulsory over these years.
  • Money & teams: Sponsor liveries allowed from 1968, starting with Lotus in Gold Leaf colours.

With the same Cosworth engine in most cars, races were won on chassis design and driving. Lotus, Tyrrell, McLaren and Brabham won titles; Ferrari came back with Niki Lauda in 1975. Speeds rose fast and many drivers were killed, which pushed through the first big safety rules.

Who got it right: The Cosworth DFV, which powered most of the champions; Lotus (four constructors' titles in this period).

1977–1982: Ground effect and the first turbos

Lotus discovered that if you shape the underside of the car like an upside-down wing and seal the sides with skirts that touch the track, the air under the car is sucked out and the car is pressed onto the road. This is ground effect. In the same year, Renault brought the first turbo engine.

  • Aerodynamics: Ground-effect 'wing cars' with sliding skirts (1977–1982). Skirts were banned in 1981, then fixed skirts allowed again in 1982.
  • Aerodynamics: Brabham's fan car of 1978, which sucked air out with a fan, won once and was withdrawn.
  • Engine: Turbo engines of 1.5 litres were allowed alongside 3-litre engines. Renault's arrived in 1977 and won from 1979.
  • Safety: Medical centres at every track and a medical car became compulsory.
  • Money & teams: The first Concorde Agreement (1981) settled the commercial deal. From 1981 every team had to build its own chassis.

Cornering speeds rose so much that drivers were badly hurt when skirts lifted or cars bottomed out. Lotus's 79 won the 1978 title; Williams won in 1980 and 1981. The turbo engines grew more powerful every year.

Who got it right: Lotus with the 78 and 79 (1977–78), then Williams (1980–81).

1983–1988: The turbo era

Ground effect was banned, and every car needed a flat bottom between the wheels. To go fast, teams turned to turbo engines, which in qualifying produced well over 1,000 horsepower. The rules then reined the turbos in step by step, with fuel limits and boost limits, until they were banned.

  • Aerodynamics: Flat bottom required from 1983. Four-wheel drive and cars with more than four wheels banned.
  • Engine: Refuelling during races banned from 1984. Race fuel limited to 220 litres (1984), 195 litres (1986) and 150 litres for turbos (1988).
  • Engine: Turbo boost limited to 4.0 bar in 1987 and 2.5 bar in 1988. 3.5-litre non-turbo engines allowed from 1987.
  • Safety: Front crash test required from 1985; catch fencing banned from 1986; Super Licence needed from 1984.

The best turbo won. Brabham-BMW won the 1983 drivers' title; McLaren with its TAG-Porsche engine and Williams-Honda dominated after. In 1988 McLaren-Honda, with Ayrton Senna and Alain Prost, won 15 of 16 races.

Who got it right: McLaren (TAG-Porsche, then Honda), Williams-Honda; Brabham's BT52 was built in weeks and won the 1983 title for Nelson Piquet.

1989–1993: 3.5 litres and electronic wizardry

Turbos were banned, and every car used a 3.5-litre naturally aspirated engine, V8, V10 or V12. Teams found new speed in electronics: computer-controlled active suspension that kept the car level, traction control that stopped wheelspin, and semi-automatic gearboxes.

  • Engine: 3.5 litres naturally aspirated, 8 to 12 cylinders. No fuel limit.
  • Sporting rules: From 1991: 10 points for a win, and all results count.
  • Aerodynamics: Smaller front and rear wings and narrower tyres (1993) to slow the cars.
  • Safety: Higher pit walls, safety car introduced (1992), pit lane speed limits.

McLaren-Honda kept winning until 1991, then Williams-Renault, with active suspension, was so far ahead in 1992–93 that Nigel Mansell and Alain Prost won the titles easily. The computer systems were the difference.

Who got it right: McLaren-Honda (1989–91), then Williams-Renault with active suspension (1992–93).

1994–1997: Driver aids banned, safety first

For 1994 the FIA banned active suspension, traction control and anti-lock brakes (ABS) to put the driver back in charge, and brought back refuelling during races. Then at Imola in May 1994 Roland Ratzenberger and Ayrton Senna were killed. A wave of safety rules followed that shaped modern F1.

  • Sporting rules: Refuelling during pit stops returned (1994–2009).
  • Safety: After Imola: the plank under the car to raise ride height, pit lane speed limits, higher cockpit sides, stronger crash tests and slower corners at many tracks.
  • Engine: Engines cut from 3.5 to 3.0 litres in 1995; fuel had to be close to road petrol.
  • Sporting rules: 107% qualifying rule from 1996.

Benetton and Michael Schumacher won in 1994 and 1995, in a season full of controversy. Williams returned to the top in 1996–97 with Damon Hill and Jacques Villeneuve.

Who got it right: Benetton (1994–95), Williams (1996–97).

1998–2008: Narrow cars and grooved tyres

To slow cars in corners, they were made narrower (from 2 metres to 1.8 metres) and given grooved tyres with less rubber on the road. From 2000 engines had to be 3-litre V10s. Bridgestone and Michelin fought a tyre war, and qualifying formats kept changing.

  • Aerodynamics: Car width cut to 1.8 m; grooved tyres (three grooves at the front, four from 1999).
  • Engine: 3-litre V10s (2000–2005), then 2.4-litre V8s from 2006 with a 19,000 rpm rev limit (2007).
  • Tyres: Bridgestone vs Michelin until 2006; Bridgestone sole supplier from 2007. No tyre changes in races in 2005.
  • Sporting rules: Points to eighth place from 2003 (10, 8, 6, 5, 4, 3, 2, 1). Knockout qualifying (Q1, Q2, Q3) from 2006.
  • Safety: HANS device compulsory from 2003; accident data recorders from 1997.
  • Engine: Traction control re-allowed in 2001, banned for good in 2008 with a standard ECU.

Mika Häkkinen won the first two drivers' titles for McLaren-Mercedes. Then Ferrari, with Schumacher, Jean Todt, Ross Brawn and Rory Byrne, won six constructors' titles in a row (1999–2004) and Schumacher five drivers' titles. Renault and Fernando Alonso broke the run in 2005–06.

Who got it right: Ferrari (1999–2004, 2007–08), McLaren (1998), Renault (2005–06).

2009–2013: Slicks, KERS and the double diffuser

The aerodynamic rules were rewritten to make overtaking easier: wide, low front wings, narrow, tall rear wings, and almost all the small winglets banned. Slick tyres returned, and KERS added a hybrid boost. Refuelling was banned again from 2010, and DRS arrived in 2011.

  • Aerodynamics: Simpler bodywork, slicks back. A loophole allowed a 'double diffuser', which Brawn used to win.
  • Engine: KERS (2009, 2011–2013): about 80 hp for about 6.7 seconds a lap. Rev limit 18,000 rpm. Eight engines per season.
  • Sporting rules: Refuelling banned from 2010. Points 25, 18, 15, 12, 10, 8, 6, 4, 2, 1 from 2010. DRS from 2011. Pirelli sole tyre supplier from 2011.
  • Money & teams: Testing during the season banned; limits on wind tunnel and CFD.

Brawn GP, rescued from Honda's ashes, won both titles in 2009 with Jenson Button. Then Red Bull and designer Adrian Newey mastered exhaust-blown diffusers and Sebastian Vettel won four titles in a row (2010–13).

Who got it right: Brawn GP (2009), Red Bull (2010–13).

2014–2021: The turbo-hybrid era

The biggest engine change in F1 history: 1.6-litre V6 turbo engines combined with two electric motor-generators (MGU-K and MGU-H) and a battery. Together they are called the power unit. Fuel was limited to 100 kg per race. In 2017 cars were made wider and faster again, and in 2018 the halo was added above the cockpit.

  • Engine: 1.6-litre V6 turbo hybrid with MGU-K and MGU-H, fuel flow limit, 100 kg (later 110 kg) of fuel per race, a limited number of power units per season.
  • Aerodynamics: 2017: cars 2 m wide again, with wider tyres and more downforce; lap records fell.
  • Safety: Halo compulsory from 2018.
  • Sporting rules: Drivers choose permanent numbers (2014); penalty points on licences; a point for fastest lap from 2019.
  • Money & teams: Cost cap from 2021 (US$145 million); aerodynamic testing on a sliding scale.

Mercedes had prepared years ahead and won the constructors' title every year from 2014 to 2021, with Lewis Hamilton taking six drivers' titles and Nico Rosberg one. Max Verstappen and Red Bull-Honda won the 2021 drivers' title in the final race.

Who got it right: Mercedes, the longest run of constructors' titles in F1 history (eight in a row).

2022–2025: Ground effect returns

Cars were redesigned so they could follow each other more closely. Most downforce now comes from tunnels under the floor (ground effect again) instead of complicated bodywork, which leaves less dirty air for the car behind. Wheels grew from 13 to 18 inches. The cost cap and wind-tunnel limits kept spending down.

  • Aerodynamics: Ground-effect floors with Venturi tunnels, simpler wings, bargeboards gone.
  • Tyres: 18-inch wheels with low-profile tyres.
  • Aerodynamics: Porpoising (bouncing) troubled many teams in 2022; floors were raised in 2023.
  • Sporting rules: Sprints from 2021, reshaped in 2023–24. Fastest-lap point dropped after 2024.

Red Bull, with Newey's design, mastered the new floors: Verstappen won four titles in a row (2021–24) and in 2023 the team won 21 of 22 races. McLaren then caught up and won the constructors' title in 2024 and 2025, with Lando Norris drivers' champion in 2025.

Who got it right: Red Bull (2022–23), then McLaren (2024–25).

2026: The new era

For 2026 both the car and engine rules changed at once. Cars are smaller and lighter, with moving wings (active aero) that open on straights. The engine loses its MGU-H, and the electric motor grows to 350 kW, so about half the power is electric. DRS is gone; a driver close behind gets extra electric energy through overtake mode. Engines run on sustainable fuel.

  • Aerodynamics: Active aero: wings switch between corner mode (downforce) and straight mode (low drag). Less downforce and much less drag.
  • Aerodynamics: Smaller cars: wheelbase 3.4 m (was 3.6), width 1.9 m (was 2.0), minimum weight 768 kg (was 800).
  • Engine: 1.6-litre V6 turbo plus a 350 kW MGU-K; MGU-H removed; fully sustainable fuel.
  • Sporting rules: Overtake mode, boost and recharge replace DRS. 22 cars with Cadillac as the 11th team.
  • Money & teams: New makers: Audi (taking over Sauber), Ford with Red Bull, Honda with Aston Martin. Cost cap raised to US$215 million as more costs were counted.

As in 2014, the team that prepared best has a head start. Mercedes has led 2026, with Kimi Antonelli on course for the drivers' title.

Who got it right: Mercedes leads so far in 2026.

2027–2031: What comes next

The 2026 car and engine rules are meant to run until about 2030, but they are already being adjusted. In June 2026 the FIA's World Motor Sport Council agreed to shift power back toward the combustion engine. Talks have started on the engine rules after that.

  • Engine: 2027: fuel flow up 5%, combustion engine up to 420 kW, MGU-K down to 300 kW: about a 58-42 split in favour of the combustion engine.
  • Engine: 2028: fuel flow up a further 13%, combustion engine 450 kW: a 60-40 split.
  • Engine: Beyond 2030: the FIA president wants V8 engines on sustainable fuel with a small hybrid part, possibly from 2030 or 2031. Not agreed yet; F1 hopes to have a plan by the end of 2026.
  • Sporting rules: The 2027 calendar has 24 races, with Portugal and Türkiye returning. A race in Rwanda is planned for the early 2030s.

Nothing beyond 2028 is fixed. This section will be updated as rules are agreed.
